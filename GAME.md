# El juego — Granada

**Status: design agreed, mockup playable on the real map, nothing built into
the app yet. El Centro and El Mercado are written — 24 missions of 122.**

Read this before touching the game. It carries every decision made so far and
why, so a fresh agent on a different machine can pick it up cold.

- **Play the mockup:** https://claude.ai/code/artifact/52e5240b-857b-4ad1-853b-b6cc1e0040fa
- **Its source:** `mockups/granada.html` in this repo — one self-contained HTML
  file, no build, no dependencies. Open it in a browser and it runs.

---

## What it is

You moved to Granada and you live there. There is no plot. You walk the city
top-down, find people with a gold speech bubble, and talk to them by
**building Spanish sentences out of chunks**. Missions are the ordinary
business of living in a Nicaraguan city. Winning is doing all of them.

It goes in the **Scenes slot on the bottom tab bar** — Scenes is already a tile
on Today, so the tab is redundant. `TAB_DEFS` in `docs/js/ui.js` builds the bar.

**The plan lives in the content repo**, `scenicprints/fluidez-es-ni`:

- `content/plan/game-spine.json` — all **122 missions** across **12 districts**,
  plus the 90-strong street crowd and the everyday phrases that must recur.
- `content/plan/GAME-PROGRESS.md` — regenerated by `game_stage.py` on every
  run, so it can never be stale. Nobody writes it by hand.
- `content/game/` — the missions themselves. **24 written so far: all of
  El Centro and all of El Mercado.** Xalteva is next.

```bash
python .github/scripts/game_stage.py --root .    # in fluidez-es-ni
```

## Decisions, and why

**OPEN WORLD. Nothing is locked.** You can walk into any district on the first
day and talk to anybody. Tier is a difficulty label that decides authoring
order and what the game suggests next — it is never a gate. Walk into a
tier-five conversation on day one and lose it if you like.

**NO PUZZLES.** Kevin: *"Dont make the missions too hard where you really need
to think through a puzzle. It's a language learning game, not a Zelda game."*
Being lost is a nudge, not a maze — ask somebody, get pointed, go. The
difficulty lives in the Spanish and nowhere else, which is also why the crowd
hints are written to be obvious rather than cryptic.

**The crowd is the quest system.** There are no map markers, and Granada has no
usable street names — a direction here is *"de la iglesia, dos cuadras al
lago"*. So 90 street NPCs point you at things, and `game_stage.py` **fails any
mission that is written but that nobody in the street points at**, because an
unfindable mission may as well not exist.

**No collecting, and no story arc.** Both were proposed and both were cut. The
premise is the plain one: you live here.

**Top-down tile world, not isometric and not 3D.** Kevin asked for "almost 3D,
or isometric, or I'd even settle for top down like classic Zelda". Three.js
cannot be CDN-loaded inside an artifact and hand-written WebGL is a poor bet
for a mockup, so top-down came first. The engine is tile-based, so isometric is
a re-skin of the renderer, not a rewrite, if he wants it later.

**Nothing is locked behind vocabulary.** The first design gated dialogue lines
on words you had already learned elsewhere. Kevin killed it: *"I dont like that
you have to go grind to play the game. What if you want to mainly use the game
to learn?"* He is right. So instead **the help fades**:

| times you have met the phrase | what you get |
|---|---|
| 0 | the **ingredients**, shuffled — `quiero` (I want), `un cuarto` (a room) — and the English of what you are trying to say |
| 1 | the English only |
| 2+ | nothing but the situation |

Get it wrong and the help comes back. Miss twice and three options appear, so
you are never stuck. That means the game can be somebody's *only* way in.

**Never show the finished sentence.** The first version of this handed you the
whole line to copy and Kevin caught it immediately: *"It doesnt make sense that
the answer is right there though. I dont even have to try."* He is right —
copying a line you can see is transcription, not recall, and there is no
learning in it. Giving the ingredients instead means a total beginner can still
attempt it with no prior Spanish, but they have to **assemble** it, which is
the actual skill. There is always an "I am stuck — show me the line" link, so
the finished sentence is a choice you make rather than the default state.

**You BUILD the answer from chunks. You do not type it.** Typing was tried and
Kevin killed it: *"We cant be doing typed answers. Because you have to type
exactly how it is programmed. And the user may type something, and technically
it is correct, but the program does know."* That is the real failure of free
text and no amount of fuzzy matching closes it — a learner who is right and
gets marked wrong stops playing.

So the answer is assembled from a tray of chunks: `Buenas` `quiero`
`un cuarto` `mañana`. Tap to place, tap again to take back. This is **not**
multiple choice — you are not picking one of three finished sentences, you are
composing one, which is the skill. It also means accents cost nothing and there
is no phone keyboard to fight.

**No speech recognition, and NO SPEECH AT ALL.** Two separate calls:
- Recognition was never on the table. `docs/js/speech.js` already says why:
  *"browser speech recognition is unreliable on iPhone and on regional
  accents"*, which is why Shadowing records and plays back but never scores.
  An es-MX recognizer would mark him **wrong for pronouncing things
  Nicaraguan** — aspirated s, *vamoh*, *pueh*.
- Synthesis was in the first mockup and **Kevin cut it: "We need to drop the
  vocal completely. It is terrible. The game will be reading only."** The only
  voice available is es-MX reading Nicaraguan Spanish in a Mexican accent. All
  `speechSynthesis` code is out of the mockup. Do not put it back.

**The map is the real city, surveyed — the WHOLE city, not the core.** Kevin:
*"we need to build and mapout the real Granada. Use some internet source for
the real granada. We are doing the ENTIRE city."* So it is OpenStreetMap
geometry, pulled from Overpass and rasterised into tiles by
`scripts/osm_granada.py`: **1089 × 885 tiles, five metres to a tile, 5.4 × 4.4
km**. The colonial grid, La Calzada, Parque Central with the cathedral on its
east side, the Mercado Municipal, Xalteva, Guadalupe, La Merced, the cemetery,
Fortaleza La Pólvora, the stadium, the old train station, Transnica, the
cauces, and the shore of Cocibolca with the isletas. Every district on the
spine has a real place to stand in.

Google Maps was considered and rejected: billing on file, costs past a free
tier, terms awkward about games (they killed their gaming SDK in 2021), and an
embed would look like a Google Map dropped into the app. OSM has none of those
problems and it is survey data, not imagery — nothing is redistributed, the
geometry is redrawn as tiles.

**The houses in the blocks are generated, and that is deliberate.** OSM has all
of Granada's streets and 685 of its buildings — hotels, churches and schools in
the tourist core, and almost nothing else. Rendered honestly you get the real
grid running between empty lots, which reads as a city that was demolished.
So `infill_blocks()` builds the frontage each street really has: deep at
Parque Central, thinning to the edge of town, ragged at the back, yards and
fruit trees in the middle of each block. It never overwrites a surveyed
building, a park, a pitch or the water. **The grid you navigate by is
surveyed; the wall you walk past is plausible.**

**The tile grid is baked in Python, not rasterised in the browser.** The
obvious design ships vectors and rasterises at boot, which means writing the
rasteriser twice — once so a PNG can be looked at, once so the game can draw —
and the two drift. One rasteriser, in the generator. What the preview PNG
shows is what the game draws.

**Granada moves.** Kevin: *"vehicles should be driving throughout the map."*
Caponeras (the three-wheeled mototaxis that are what public transport here
actually is), motos, bicis, taxis, camionetas, buses on the main roads and a
horse carriage in the tourist streets, all driving **the same street graph the
tiles were painted from** — so a caponera turns where there is really a corner.
Twenty-six are simulated at a time, near you, recycled onto an off-screen
street when they get more than about a screen and a half away.

They **brake for you and honk, and they never hit you**. There is no damage,
no fail state and nothing to lose in the street, because a language game that
punishes you for standing in the road teaches you to avoid the place where all
the people are. You cannot walk through one, which is the whole of the
interaction.

**Fourteen of the 122 missions need a vehicle you can talk to** — the cobrador
on the bus, the taxista, the colectivo, the lancha out to the isletas, the moto
workshop. Those are parked vehicles with a gold bubble, not traffic.

**It stays inside Fluidez, on the phone.** Kevin asked whether it should be a
standalone desktop app. No: a separate app cannot see your vocabulary, streak
or fluency, which all live in Fluidez already — and that link is the best thing
the game has. It is also a ten-minutes-in-a-queue game, not a sit-at-a-desk
one. "Too big" gets solved by shipping one district, not by changing platform.

**Granada, even though the course is Managua barrio Spanish.** Granada is where
a learner actually lands, it is walkable and legible, and the fact that people
there will answer a chele in English is not a problem — it is a mission.

## How answers are graded

**Exact match, and that is the whole grader.** Because the tray is authored,
the set of sentences a player can produce is finite and known, so every correct
assembly can simply be listed. Nothing a player can build is
correct-but-refused. That is the entire reason for the chunk tray.

The comparison still normalises first — lowercase, accents stripped,
punctuation stripped — so the `ok` lists can be written in plain ASCII.

Two invariants, both checked by `mockups/checkbeats.py`, and both worth
re-running whenever a beat is written or edited:

1. The tiles laid down **in their written order** must be one of the accepted
   answers, or the beat is unwinnable.
2. Every accepted answer must be **buildable from the chunks the tray offers**,
   or it is dead data pretending to be a second right answer.

Each beat carries `tiles` (the chunks that build the answer), `extra` (chunks
that do not), and `ok` (every assembly that counts). The **help ladder is now
noise**, not the answer:

| times you have met the phrase | tray | told what you are saying? |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | tiles + 1 distractor | yes |
| 1 | tiles + 2 distractors | yes |
| 2+ | tiles + 4 distractors | no |

Miss twice and it lays the right answer out in the tray for you, so a beat can
never dead-end. There is also an always-available "I am stuck — show me the
line".

Copy `.github/scripts/game_write_mercado.py` in the content repo and edit it.
Emit a whole batch from one throwaway Python file; never one tool call per
mission. Every beat needs `es` (what they say), `objective`, `key` (the phrase
the help ladder counts), `say` (the target), `en`, `tiles`, `extra`, `ok`,
`teaches` and `good` (why the right answer is right). Every mission needs a
`culture` note. Then add crowd lines in `content/game/crowd/<district>.json`
pointing at each new mission, or `game_stage.py` will fail them as unfindable.

Then, in the content repo:

```bash
python .github/scripts/game_stage.py --root .      # is it playable and findable
python .github/scripts/dialect_game.py --root .    # is the Spanish Nicaraguan
```

Fix what they report and commit only when neither prints a `PROBLEM:` line.
`git pull --rebase` before pushing.

Then bake the new missions into the mockup, over in this repo:

```bash
python scripts/game_bake.py --content ../fluidez-es-ni
node mockups/checkworld.js
```

`game_bake.py` needs a placement for each new mission — a real named place and
an offset in tiles, in the `PLACE` table at the top of it. Anything without
one lands at its district anchor and is reported rather than silently stacked
on top of the last mission that did the same.

**2. Then work outward.** Twelve districts, 122 missions.

**3. The app screen** — the canvas world, the chunk tray, and swapping Scenes
out of `TAB_DEFS`. This is the only part that needs an app release. Do it once
enough content exists to be worth playing, not before.

**4. The wiring that justifies all of it.** `teaches` on every beat must
resolve to real dictionary lemmas, so playing feeds the same vocabulary the
reader and the drills feed. Order a coffee in Granada and `café` gets stronger
in Words; neglect it and it fades. Without that link this is a nice separate
game. With it, it is a way into the course.

### The mockup

`mockups/granada.html` — one self-contained file, no build. Published at
https://claude.ai/code/artifact/52e5240b-857b-4ad1-853b-b6cc1e0040fa

**Kevin has played it and it works** — walking, talking, and the chunk tray.
Note that neither browser tool available here can deliver a keypress or a click
into a sandboxed artifact iframe, and the in-app pane will not run a live local
page, so you cannot drive it yourself. Verify by reading and let Kevin play.

`mockups/checkbeats.py` checks the beats **inside the mockup file**;
`game_stage.py` checks the real content. They are not the same script.

**`node mockups/checkworld.js` checks the city.** It loads granada.html in
Node behind a stub DOM and asserts the things you cannot see by reading: that
the map survived packing and injection (the generator ships a checksum line
for exactly this), that the solid tiles agree on both sides, that the street
graph joins up, that a thousand frames of traffic leaves nobody in a wall or
off the map, that the lake is east of Parque Central, that traffic brakes and
honks for somebody standing in the road, and that no NPC is standing inside a
building. It has already caught two real bugs. **Run it after any change to
the map or the traffic** — it is the only way to test this from here.

Rebuilding the map:

```bash
python scripts/osm_granada.py --preview out/    # rebuild from the cache, PNGs to look at
python scripts/osm_granada.py --fetch           # pull fresh from Overpass first
```

The Overpass instances shed load constantly, so `--fetch` retries across
mirrors and refuses to cache an answer with fewer than 400 streets in it —
one mirror in that list served a Switzerland extract, answered 200 OK with
almost nothing, and blanked the cache. `--raw` rebuilds from Overpass JSON
already on disk. `reference/granada-osm.json` is the committed extract, so
none of this needs the network on a normal day.

Rough edges Kevin has not complained about, so do not spend time here yet:
every facade faces south, ALTTP-style, so houses on the south side of a street
show their front to the next block; and there is no street life on foot —
nobody wandering, no dog, no washing on a line. The traffic is the only thing
moving.

## Other open threads, unrelated to the game

- **`scenicprints/fluidez-gsw-lu`** — Kevin chose "delete it permanently" and
  was asked to confirm once, since GitHub cannot undo it. **Not yet
  confirmed, not yet deleted.** The languages registry already lists only
  `es-ni`, so nothing in the app can reach it either way.
- **The voseo imperative is not drillable.** `startVerbs()` in
  `docs/js/screens.js` draws a random subject index 0–4 for whatever tense it
  picks, and the imperative has one form and no subject, so it would hand the
  drill an `undefined`. `verbs.json` already carries an `imperative` key per
  irregular verb. Approved, not started. Note `engine.js conjugate()` falls
  back to the regular table when a tense is missing, so `verbs.regular` needs
  an `imperative` row or regular verbs will silently conjugate wrong — that is
  exactly the bug that had the trainer teaching "cerro" and "perdo" for years.
- **The course itself is done and published** — 185 stories, 95 scenes, 52
  patterns, 97.9% of words on the page tappable. See `NEXT.md` in
  `scenicprints/fluidez-es-ni`.
